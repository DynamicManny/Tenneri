What I build

My design philosophy comes down to one question: if the learner had the choice, would they stay?

Courses people stay in

When I was brought in to review an AI workforce program, learners were skipping their LMS assignments to stay in DataCamp, because that's where the hands-on practice was. So I rebuilt the course as one place to learn, with lessons, practice and a working terminal on the same page. Nobody needed to leave unless it was for further reading.

Hard subjects, minus the overwhelm

One 16-week course had to cover quantum computing, AI and cybersecurity, and any one of those can bury a beginner. Instead of three separate tracks, I overlapped them. Quantum gets explained through a security or AI lens, so every new idea leans on one learners already have.

Games where the mechanic is the lesson

If it's a racing game, a quiz shouldn't pop up mid-race and kill the momentum. Answer well between races and you earn better upgrades for your car. When the learning feeds the mechanic, nobody feels like they're being tested.

How I work

Nobody taught me the industry way, so I built my own. I learned the names for most of it, like backward design and scenario-based learning, after I was already doing it.

  1. Look before I read

    The first time I open a course, I don't read a word. I look at how the text sits on the page. Is it wordy? Are there dense blocks nobody will get through? A course can be ugly and still feel great to get through, so the feel comes first.

  2. Ask the people who hire the graduates

    On that same AI program, graduates knew the material but struggled to use it. I rebuilt the course with input from workforce managers, and the next cohort came out more technical. Managers still saw a gap. They had told us the gap existed, never how deep it went. So I sat with the placement managers, got specific about every missing skill, and built the next version around their answers.

  3. Make it interesting, then teach

    I was failing math until a teacher started drawing the concepts on the board, and it finally clicked. So when I had to teach cybersecurity, I drew too. Unfortunately, I'm not artistically gifted. Bob was a stick man with a beard, and when he needed a girlfriend, I copied Bob and gave him a wig. My students started calling me Picasso. They laughed, but the concepts stuck.

  4. Why code and not Storyline?

    The honest answer: I didn't know Articulate existed. I figured everything out by experimenting. When I finally tried Storyline, it felt limiting next to what I could already build by hand, so I went back to pushing the LMS to its limits. You get every file, and nothing needs a license to keep running.

I got here by accident.

I wrote stories and sermons for my church magazine in elementary school. In high school the arts side of things was basically gone, and I felt lost for a while. At 18 I applied to psychology because a friend said I was good at listening. Then I switched to criminology because a dean said I should. That was the pattern for a stretch: trying to move forward while feeling disconnected from the work.

In 2024 I misread a work email. "Tour of duties." I thought it meant a trip to the head office in Toronto. That tour is where I first saw instructional design. It changed everything.

I was an instructor at the time. The content was dry. An AI course with uninspired material. Then I found a source code feature in the LMS and realized I could write HTML, CSS, and JavaScript myself. I barely knew what I was doing. I taught myself for the next cohort anyway. The early work is rough. I cringe looking at it now. But something opened up. Pages turned into labs. Labs turned into full environments. Eventually I built two browser-based operating systems inside the LMS: PonchOS (a pun on my university nickname, Poncho) and ROSe.

ROSe, the Ramano Operating System Emulator

An operating system, built inside Brightspace.

A browser-based OS that runs inside Brightspace. I built it because the course needed a place to apply skills, not another set of slides.

The framework behind it

Fluid Adaptive Runtime (FAR)

Every environment I build runs on FAR, a proprietary framework for creating interactive LMS content that adapts to each learner, grades itself, and never needs a vendor to keep working.

Fluid
Same environment, different experience for every person. Scenarios branch, variables shift, no two learners take the same path.
Adaptive
Completion data and decision paths flow back to the gradebook automatically. The assessment is built into the experience.
Runtime
Everything executes in the browser. No external server calls, no vendor dependencies. If the LMS runs, it runs.
Why 2.0 is restrained

Real incident response isn't flashy.

ROSe 2.0 is narrative and graded through SCORM. You log in to find your company has been breached, and you have to fix it. It's deliberately restrained, because real incident response at the C-suite level isn't flashy. The story keeps it interesting.
